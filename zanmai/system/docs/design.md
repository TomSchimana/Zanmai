# Designing documents

Flyers, one-pagers, slide decks and longer documents built in your organisation's own visual language.

## What Zanmai needs from you

The brand cannot be guessed, so it is read from real material. Have ready at least one existing piece in the look you want, a flyer, a deck, a template file, plus the logo and any imagery that should be used. Without a template Zanmai asks for one instead of inventing a house style.

Three things are settled with you before it starts: who the piece is for, what it should achieve, and whether you want a **copy** of an existing template with new content or a **new piece** composed in the same visual language. Copy is for "same as this one"; composing is the default for "make me a flyer about X".

## How the piece is built

Your templates are read for their concrete values: the exact colours, type sizes, spacing rhythm, block geometry and recurring motifs. Those become a reusable kit for that brand and format, kept in the vault rather than next to the finished file, which is why a second piece in the same style comes faster than the first and why the third is better than the second.

Part of that kit is a limit on how many forms each element may have: two kinds of quote, not five. It sounds like a restriction and it is the opposite. A reader recognises structure because things repeat, so a document where every quote looks different has no quote form at all, only forty individual decisions. Where a case fits none of the existing forms, one form is widened for every place it appears rather than a new one invented on the spot.

The structure then follows your content rather than the template's boxes. Several things compared on the same attributes become a table, repeated items of the same shape become a grid whose count comes from the number of items, a sequence becomes a flow. That is the difference between a designed piece and a template with text dropped into it, where blocks end up two-thirds empty.

While building, Zanmai renders the piece and looks at the result instead of trusting its own description of it, then fixes what it sees. It checks the measurable things itself: nothing overflowing or colliding, no empty block, contrast sufficient, the brand's real values used. The counting parts are not left to the eye at all but run as a check: how many forms each element has grown, colours or sizes that are in neither your brand nor the kit, elements that would break across a column, and how far down each page the content actually reaches. Red means not finished, however good the page looks. The taste call it does not make for you. You get a preview and decide.

## A longer piece is agreed from a few pages first

For anything past a handful of pages you decide on a proof before the effort goes in. For separate pieces, a flyer or a deck, that is five surfaces: the opening one, an ordinary one, and the hardest cases your content brings.

A long flowing document works differently, because where a page ends depends on everything before it, so five hand-picked pages would not be real pages. There the whole document is built through once, deliberately rough, and you get real pages with their real numbers plus a contact sheet of every page, so you see the shape of the whole rather than five excerpts. Building and rendering are the cheap part; the expensive part is the polishing, and that is what waits for your word.

With it comes the check result as it came out, what the run has cost so far and what the rest would cost. Say go and the same run continues with everything it already worked out. Say "almost, but" and the change goes into the kit, so it holds for all the pages rather than being patched on the one where you noticed it. This is the difference between one round of feedback and sixty pages polished twice.

## What you get

By default a rendered page: a file for the screen, or a print-ready PDF with the fonts embedded and verified rather than merely referenced. Page size comes from context, A4 in Europe, Letter in the US, or whatever the piece calls for. Everything lands in `doing`, one folder per piece, ready to pull out. The drafts stay beside the finished file, because they are the same matter.

When you need a file you can keep editing yourself, the piece is built natively in Affinity or PowerPoint instead. Say so up front, because it changes how it is made.

Which tool builds it follows from what the piece has to be able to do, not from what is quickest to drive. Anything paged, a card as much as a manual, is set with a typesetting engine, because that is what makes text flow across pages and moves a full-width element that no longer fits instead of leaving half a page white. Zanmai fetches that engine itself at first use, one self-contained file, and proves it works on a test document before using it on yours. A browser is used when the deliverable is a web page.

Two limits are named rather than worked around. A real press run with an exact colour profile, bleed and crop marks belongs in a native tool, and so does a file you will keep editing by hand; a CMYK copy can be produced, but this is not a prepress step. Whatever a job needs is checked before it starts rather than halfway through.

## What never happens

Your originals are not touched; Zanmai works on copies and writes new files, and a text you wrote stays exactly as you wrote it in your own notes. Inside the piece being set, wording is fitted to the layout the way a designer and a writer would settle it at one desk, a shorter word, a better break, a caption cut to its line, and you get that list with the result. Anything that would change what a sentence means is put to you instead of decided by the layout. Colours and fonts come from your material, never from memory or approximation. Content is never quietly dropped to make a layout problem disappear, and if something does not fit you are told. No image is generated behind your back either: missing imagery is raised with you first, together with the cost, because generating spends money and commits the piece to AI imagery.
